Small-business software · Client usability study

Sharing usability findings while the product could still change

A client wanted to know whether small-business owners would see value in its new software and find it easy to use. I helped plan and moderate a ten-participant study. After five sessions the same issues kept recurring, so we shared early findings and the client could consider revisions before the rest of the study.

01 · The research approach

Designing sessions to test understanding, not just task completion

The client’s questions were about value and clarity as much as usability. Watching people complete tasks would show whether they could use it. Asking why would show whether they understood it.

I wrote · Recruiting script and screening

Finding participants who matched the product’s real audience

I wrote the recruiting script and reviewed participants against the client’s criteria: small-business ownership or decision-making responsibility, across a range of revenue levels, locations and industries.

I developed · Moderation guide

Starting with onboarding, before any task

I developed the guide’s tasks, prompts and follow-up questions. Opening with onboarding let us see whether participants understood the product’s purpose, not only whether they could operate it.

How each session was structured

1. Onboarding

Do participants understand what the product is for?

2. Scenario-based task

Can they apply what they learned in a realistic task?

3. Interview

How do they describe its value, ease of use and clarity in their own words?

This order mattered later. Because comprehension came up in onboarding and again in the interview, we could see when task success and understanding didn’t line up.

02 · In the sessions

Watching what people do, then asking why

My part

I moderated sessions and participant interviews, and observed my teammate’s sessions.

A participant’s comment

“It’s dark, it’s hard to read. Depressing, kinda.”

This made me consider how the interface’s visual tone could shape how people judged the product, beyond whether they could finish a task.

When participants struggled

What happened

Several participants became frustrated during tasks.

What I did

I asked what they expected to happen and what was getting in the way.

What it gave us

Frustration showed something wasn’t working. Their explanations showed why.

03 · The evidence

By session five, the same issues kept coming back

After each session, the three of us compared notes. By participants four and five, these themes were recurring. Early patterns · 5 of 10 participants

01 · Value

Purpose wasn’t clear

Participants were unsure what it did or what they’d get from it.

02 · Tone

Game elements felt off

XP, coins and streaks felt cartoony to some participants for a business tool.

03 · Navigation

No clear starting point

Participants asked which tool to use, and in what order.

04 · Effort

Effort before payoff

Participants gave a lot of information before knowing what they’d get back.

05 · Trust

Security felt unclear

Some participants were unsure the product was secure or transparent.

The strongest pattern, from evidence to question

Observed

Several participants finished onboarding and the scenario task while still unsure what the product was for.

Interpretation

Task success didn’t mean understanding. The product’s value wasn’t landing during onboarding.

Why it matters

The client asked whether owners see value. Task success alone would have overstated it.

Question for the client

How might onboarding show what owners get back before asking for their business details?

Issues 01 and 04 point the same way: people were asked to invest time and information before they understood the payoff. That is a comprehension problem, not a small slip in a task.

The evidence

The same issues were recurring by sessions four and five, and they were about whether people understood the product at all.

The opportunity

Waiting for all ten would have meant reporting problems the client could already be working on. Sharing now gave them time to consider revisions before the remaining sessions.

Keeping the study sound

We framed the findings as early patterns from 5 of 10 participants. The study continues as planned, and later results will be read against the version each participant used.

05 · Where it stands and what I learned

Good research timing can matter as much as good findings

Status, stated plainly

Shared

We shared initial findings and recommendations with the client.

Now

The client is evaluating possible revisions. Nothing has been changed or validated yet.

Next

The remaining five round-1 sessions and round 2 with all ten participants will show whether these patterns hold and how any changes affect the experience.

This was my first usability study, and it changed how I think about the work. It isn’t only finding issues and reporting them at the end. It’s recognizing patterns across participants, understanding the reasons behind them, and deciding when the evidence is strong enough to inform a decision.

Sharing early didn’t mean lowering the bar. It meant being clear about what five sessions could and couldn’t tell the client, so they could act while there was still time.
