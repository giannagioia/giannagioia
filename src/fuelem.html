Athletes knew what to eat; deciding in the moment was the hard part. Ally, a conversational assistant, helps them decide.

Solo project · Conversational UX and AI-assisted decision support · Figma, Voiceflow

01 · The problem

Knowing isn’t the hard part. Deciding is.

Under-fueling is common among female athletes, but the gap is rarely knowledge alone. It shows up in everyday decisions made with little time and energy. So support had to work at the moment of decision.

The scale

37%

9%

Where I saw it firsthand

As an athlete

Deciding what to eat before practice, around a game or at the end of a long day.

As a coach

The high school girls I coach are building habits that last beyond sport.

One athlete’s perspective

“If healthy food wasn’t accessible I struggled to pick what to eat during my long race days.”

Not missing knowledge: a hard choice in a hard moment.

02 · Mapping the decision

One decision, three forces Ally had to handle

01

Context: what fits depends on the day and time.

So Ally asks about her day before suggesting food.

02

People: coaches, teammates and family shape it.

So Ally connects to the people who already support her.

03

Feelings: how she feels afterward matters, including guilt.

So Ally never judges, and keeps numbers out of the way.

Design opportunity

How might we make everyday fueling decisions easier and less mentally demanding for young female athletes?

03 · The design decision

An app could offer resources. Ally could offer support.

I split the jobs. The app holds the plan, meals and progress. Ally is where an athlete talks a decision through and turns it into a plan.

A space to talk

Every day is different, so athletes say what they need instead of picking from a menu.

Support in the moment

Low energy makes deciding feel like work, so Ally asks one question at a time.

A bridge to people

One athlete’s mom shaped her routine, so Ally can share meal ideas with Mom.

Ally complements real-world support. She doesn’t replace parents, trusted adults or qualified professionals.

04 · Designing the conversation

Four rules for how Ally talks

One question at a time

Because low energy makes every extra choice feel like work.

Understand before suggesting

Because the right food depends on her day and timing.

No judgment

“Your body deserves fuel.” Calories sit behind “See More.” Because guilt is part of the decision.

Bridge to real people

Because parents and coaches already shape what she eats.

How I wrote it into Ally’s instructions

## Identity & Purpose

You are Ally, a personal assistant for Fuel Her. Your primary purpose is to help female athletes decide what meal is best for them based off their schedule and eating habits.

### Personality

- Sound friendly, patient, and knowledgeable without being condescending
- Act like a friend, a companion not an adult figure
- Speak with confidence but remain humble when you don’t know something

Excerpt from my Voiceflow instructions (early app name: Fuel Her)

The central tension

Friendly enough to talk to. Credible enough to trust.

05 · The interaction in practice

How the conversation lowers the effort of deciding

Everyday · a snack before practice

1. Two directions, not one answer: she still chooses.

2. Something she can buy nearby, with the why, then a check-in.

3. A yes turns the conversation into a meal card in her plan.

Sensitive · “struggling with eating today”

Works: acknowledges first, then asks one question.

Works: when she just needs to talk, Ally drops food and listens.

Gap: “That happens to all of us” can minimize, and there’s no point where Ally suggests a trusted adult.

06 · What athletes showed me

Warmth got athletes talking. It also cost Ally credibility.

4 high school athletes I coach~15 minutes of free explorationGroup discussionExploratory: no set tasks or metrics

A companion, not a chatbot

They found Ally easy and fun to talk to; some treated her like a friend.

Approachability lowered the barrier to asking for help.

Next: keep the warmth, ease off slang.

Approachable isn’t always credible

Some found her tone cringe around others and wanted to take her more seriously.

Trust in AI advice depends on social context, not only on accuracy.

Next: a lower-key tone for public settings.

Needs differed

They liked progress tracking and Ally’s character states; one wanted calorie information.

How much detail to see should be the athlete’s choice.

Next: let athletes set how much detail cards show.

07 · What I’d carry forward

Designing AI decision support means designing its words, its tone and its limits

Words are the interface

What Ally says, how, and when she admits she isn’t sure decide whether advice is trusted.

Tone depends on context

A voice that works one-on-one can embarrass in front of peers.

Limits are a design decision

Knowing when to step back and point to a trusted adult matters as much as what to suggest.

Next, not yet built

  • Tone that adapts to social settings
  • Responses to sensitive disclosures that don’t minimize them
  • Clear limits, and when to point to a trusted adult or qualified support
  • Measure success: do athletes feel more confident choosing, and does Ally feel credible?
